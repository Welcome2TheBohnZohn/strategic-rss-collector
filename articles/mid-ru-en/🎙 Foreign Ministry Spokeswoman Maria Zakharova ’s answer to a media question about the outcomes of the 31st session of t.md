🎙
Foreign Ministry Spokeswoman
Maria Zakharova
’s
answer
to a media question about the outcomes of the 31st session of the Conference of the Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change
(November 27, 2026)
❓
Question:
The 31st session of the Conference of the Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change concluded in Antalya on November 2. How do you assess its outcomes?
💬
Maria Zakharova:
The 31st session of the Conference of the Parties to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC COP31) was marked by
a highly revealing episode:
the European Union, which portrays itself as the foremost champion of international climate cooperation, blocked the adoption of the Conference’s political decision, the Antalya Pact.
Its refusal to support a compromise that would have taken into account the interests of all participants was justified by claiming that the document lacked sufficient “ambition.” In reality, this meant that the text did not contain a provision calling for a phase-out of fossil fuels. In other words,
the desire to secure the inclusion of a single formulation of political significance
to Brussels proved more important than the consensus that the parties had spent two weeks working hard to achieve.
It is telling that the dramatic statements in Western media about the lack of ambition in the outcome of COP31 have overshadowed the fact that
the parties nevertheless managed to reach agreements
on a number of complex and practically important issues, including the Adaptation Fund, mechanisms for implementing the Paris Agreement, and other key areas of international climate cooperation.
Questions of direct relevance to the practical implementation of the Convention and the Paris Agreement, such as the provision of funding, technology transfer, adaptation, a just transition, and ensuring that developing countries have access to the means of implementation, were
pushed into the background.
👉
In other words, precisely where high-profile declarations need to give way to concrete action and the fulfilment of commitments,
European enthusiasm and ambition simply evaporated.
The EU seems to have become
so much convinced of its own exceptional role
as the leading champion of the fight against climate change that it has completely confused multilateral negotiations with the process of approving its own internal directives. Perhaps the greatest disappointment was not even the absence of the desired wording from the final document, but the realisation that
the international community is capable of finding practical solutions
without accepting European political prescriptions as binding guidelines for action.
🤷‍♀️
Consensus is welcome when everyone agrees with Brussels. When they do not, the outcomes of negotiations are declared
“inadequate.”
Against this backdrop, the role of the Turkish Presidency stood out in particularly sharp contrast. Despite the pressure brought to bear,
Ankara demonstrated a mature and responsible approach
to conducting the negotiating process, reaffirming its commitment to multilateralism not as a mere declaration, but as painstaking diplomatic work based on finding a balance, respecting different national positions, and being prepared to take into account the legitimate interests of all Parties.
Diplomacy is the art of coming to terms, not of making everyone collectively repeat one’s own slogans.
And if the sole criterion for the “success” of a Conference of the Parties is the inclusion of one coveted phrase, the problem lies in the inability to distinguish a genuine outcome from
a symbolic victory
that can conveniently be sold to the domestic European audience.
Ultimately, UNFCCC COP31 has taught Brussels
a valuable lesson:
those who turn negotiations into a campaign to impose their own wording risk going down in history not as leaders of the climate process, but as its main obstacle.
