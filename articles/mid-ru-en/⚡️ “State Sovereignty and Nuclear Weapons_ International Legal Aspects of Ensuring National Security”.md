Developments surrounding Iran
and the ongoing debate over
Russia’s right to use nuclear weapons
in the course of
the special military operation
give us cause to ask how firmly established
the legal foundations of nuclear non-proliferation
are today, and whether they are set in stone.
Key points
:
👉
A state’s right to ensure its own survival under international law
It is worth recalling
the International Court of Justice’s 1996 advisory opinion
on
the Legality of the Use by a State of Nuclear Weapons in Armed Conflict
. The Court stated that it
“cannot lose sight of the fundamental right of every State to survival, and thus its right to resort to self-defence, in accordance with
Article 51 of the UN Charter
, when its survival is at stake.”
👉
The NPT: the limits of the treaty regime and Iran’s legal status
At various times, different legal grounds have been invoked
to justify what was, in essence, a political campaign to put pressure on Iran
. The unlawfulness of this Western approach became clearest when Western countries moved from monitoring and sanctions to the use of armed force. <…>
None of these instruments authorised the US or Israel
to use armed force as a sanction for Iran’s breach of its nuclear obligations, nor did the UN Security Council issue a separate authorisation for such an operation.
This is what distinguishes the preventive measures
Russia took as part of the special military operation under
Article 51 of the UN Charter
.
The immediate grounds for launching the special military operation were
the direct threat of military action by Ukraine
, which in 2019 declared
full NATO membership
a strategic objective and in 2020 became
a NATO Enhanced Opportunities Partner
, and
the genocide perpetrated by Ukraine against its own population
in the east of the country.
☝️
As for the other threats Russia faces as the Ukrainian crisis unfolds, Kiev and those who share its views should always bear in mind that
we have the right, and in certain circumstances the duty
, to
use all means at our disposal
to safeguard national security, including, potentially, the use of nuclear weapons.
The current version of
the Basic Principles of State Policy of the Russian Federation on Nuclear Deterrence
expressly provides for this possibility
.
👉
The limits international law places on safeguarding national security today
• On July 22, 2026, the German government issued
a contradictory statement
. It pledged to honour its obligations under
the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons
(
#NPT
) and
the Two Plus Four Treaty
, while confirming that Germany would continue to participate in NATO’s nuclear deterrence arrangements and cooperate with France on
“forward deterrence”
.
This position
blurs the legal distinction
between the prohibition on Germany manufacturing, possessing or exercising control over nuclear weapons and its growing involvement in
the alliance’s nuclear policy
.
👉
The legal foundations of nuclear non-proliferation
can hardly be regarded as immutable
. Yet it is equally clear that, at this point in history,
they are the best humanity has devised
.
What undermines the universality of the
#NPT
is
the uneven way it is applied in practice for political reasons
, rather than any flaw in the text of the Treaty.
When the nuclear activities a state is permitted to pursue depend in practice on whether it is
an adversary or an ally
of a major power or a military bloc,
the rules of the non-proliferation regime lose some of their credibility
.
In formal terms, the same law applies to all, yet
political practice makes its application selective
. Today,
this contradiction poses one of the gravest threats
to the stability of the non-proliferation regime.
